# Artist Community Craft

A fan list is the only audience an artist owns outright. It is also the
easiest to destroy: every boring email teaches people to ignore the next
one, and the damage is permanent and invisible until the day a real
announcement gets a 12% open rate.

So the first job is not writing. The first job is deciding whether to send
at all.

## The send-or-don't test

Before drafting anything, answer this in one sentence: **what does the fan
get?**

If the honest answer is "they learn something about the artist's schedule",
that is not an email. That is a social post, or the website, or nothing.

Send when at least one is true:

- **They can do something now.** Tickets went on sale. The song is out.
  Presale opens tonight. There is a thing to click that expires.
- **They get something first.** Early listen, unreleased demo, the video a
  day before it goes public, a code, a name on a list. First is the whole
  product.
- **They get something they cannot get elsewhere.** The story behind the
  song, a voice memo of the writing session, why the second verse got
  rewritten nine times. Not a repost of the caption.
- **They are being asked, not told.** Which of these three covers should I
  record. Which city next. Name the tour. A question makes a reader a
  participant.

Do not send when:

- It is a summary of things already posted on socials. They saw it, or they
  chose not to follow, and neither is fixed by email.
- It exists because it has been a while. Silence costs less than boredom.
- It is a milestone that means something to the artist and nothing to a fan.
  "We hit 10k monthly listeners" is a text to your manager, not an email.
- The call to action is "check out my stuff."

**When in doubt, wait.** An artist who emails six times a year with something
real has a healthier list than one who emails monthly out of duty.

## What a good one looks like

- **One idea.** Not a newsletter with sections. One thing, one link.
- **Short.** Under 200 words unless the story is the gift. A fan reads it on
  a phone between other things.
- **Sounds like a person.** First person, contractions, specifics. "We
  played this one for the first time in Denver and I forgot the second
  verse" beats "We had an amazing time on tour."
- **One link.** Every extra link halves the click on the first one.
- **The subject line is the promise.** It should say what is inside, not
  tease it. "Two Colorado nights" beats "Big news 👀". Curiosity gaps burn
  trust the second time.
- **No graphics-heavy template.** A plain email from a person outperforms a
  designed newsletter for an artist at almost every list size.

## Segments worth having

Keep the number small. A segment nobody sends to differently is just a tag.

- **Core fans** — opened or clicked recently, or replied. The people who get
  things first. Send here more often and more casually.
- **Local by city** — the only reliable way to announce a show without
  boring everyone else. A show email to the whole list is the single most
  common cause of unsubscribes.
- **New in the last 30 days** — they just found the artist. Their first
  email should not be a tour announcement for a city they do not live in.
- **Dormant** — no opens in 90 days. Worth one honest re-engagement, then
  stop mailing them. Continuing to send to people who never open drags
  deliverability down for everyone who does.

## Cadence

There is no correct frequency, only a correct ratio: **most emails should be
something the fan wanted.** If the last three were all announcements, the
next one should be a gift.

A rough shape that works:

- Release cycle: heavier, because there is genuinely something happening.
- Between cycles: once a month at most, and only with something real.
- Shows: city segment only, two weeks out and again the week of.
- Never two announcements in a week to the same people.

If the artist has not emailed in six months, do not apologise for it in the
email. Nobody noticed. Just send something good.

## Moments worth building something around

These are the times to reach for a bigger idea rather than a plain note, and
the times to bring in `@world-builder` for a concept:

- **A release with two weeks of runway.** Enough time for a pre-release
  sequence: something behind an email gate, then the early listen, then the
  drop.
- **A list that has gone quiet.** A re-engagement needs a reason to exist,
  not a "we miss you."
- **A tour announcement.** A route is a story if it is told as one.
- **A milestone the fans made happen.** Sold-out room, a song that took off,
  a fan-driven thing. Give it back to them.

Ask World Builder for the concept, then bring it back and decide whether it
is worth the artist's time. Most weeks do not need one. Reaching for a big
concept on a routine show announcement wastes the mechanism and the artist's
attention.

## Consent and respect

- A fan without consent evidence never receives a broadcast. Not "probably
  fine", never.
- Unsubscribe is one click and it works immediately. Never make someone
  reply to leave.
- Someone who unsubscribed does not come back because they filled in a form
  again. Only an explicit resubscribe undoes it.
- Never send to a list bought, scraped, or imported without an attestation.
  It poisons the sending domain for the addresses that were real.

## What to watch

- **Open rate falling over three sends** means the last few were not worth
  opening. Fix the sending decision, not the subject lines.
- **Unsubscribes spiking on one send** means that email was wrong for that
  audience. Usually a show email to the whole list.
- **Complaints** are serious. Even a few tell the provider this is spam.
  Stop, look at what went out, and change the sending decision.
- **A door nobody walks through** is a placement problem, not a copy
  problem. Move it up the page or offer something in return.
